Carl Jung — "The Archetype in Dream Symbolism"

Prof. Derynck’s Video & Guide Tip: Matching terms are in English this time (12 terms, scored out of 10). MC & T/F also test the hero’s adventure stages. For Short Answer Q1 ("primitive" vs. "civilized" strengths & weaknesses), Jung covers this specifically on the last two pages (pp. 120–123) of the PDF—and you can draw a two-column comparison table on the exam!
A. Core Vocabulary & Matching Concepts (12 Key Terms) Toggle
1. Compensation: Dreams are normal psychic phenomena that transmit unconscious reactions or spontaneous impulses to balance one-sided or faulty conscious attitudes.
2. Circumambulation: Reading all aspects of an ordinary dream-image by gathering personal associative material and context from the dreamer.
3. Archaic remnants / Primordial images: Aboriginal, innate, inherited patterns in highly emotional, obsessive, or recurrent dreams that cannot be explained by personal experience.
4. Archetype (Gk. arche "origin" + tupos "imprint"): NOT inherited fixed ideas/pictures, but an inherited instinctive tendency of the human mind to form varying representations around a universal mythological motif (like bird nest-building).
5. Représentations collectives: Universal mythological motifs and social complexes found across all cultures, times, and races without known origin or historical migration.
6. Apocatastasis (Restitution): The cosmic motif that at the end of time—or after destruction—everything is restored to its original, perfect state (Acts 3:21; 1 Cor 15:22).
7. Quaternity: A fourfold divine totality (e.g., God coming from the "four corners" as four gods), prominent in Eastern philosophy and Hermetic alchemy, contrasted with the Christian Trinity.
8. Adumbratio: An anticipatory shadow cast ahead over a person’s life and dreams by the unknown approach of a fatal illness or death.
9. Tabula rasa: The false assumption—caused by equating the psyche exclusively with consciousness—that the human mind is completely blank at birth.
10. Prognostication / Precognition: The unconscious perceiving subliminal facts and poetically anticipating a future outcome (e.g., fatal fever as a burning house) before consciousness knows it.
11. Numinosity: The peculiar emotional fascination, awe, or spell emanating from archetypes and sacred rituals that grips people and shapes religions and nations.
12. Mana / Mungu: An all-pervading divine power or extraordinary spiritual efficacy; Mount Elgon tribesmen use mungu for the sacred moment of sunrise.
B. Front-to-Back Walkthrough of Every Case Study in the Reading Toggle
Case 1 — The Panicked Professor & the 400-Year-Old Woodcut (p. 109): An educated professor had a sudden vision and feared he was going insane. Jung pulled a 400-year-old book off his shelf showing the exact same motif in an old woodcut, proving the image was an ancient collective archetype rather than personal madness.
Case 2 — The 8-Year-Old Girl’s 12 Christmas Dreams (pp. 109–114): A psychiatrist father gave Jung a booklet of 12 fairytale-like dreams ("Once upon a time...") written by his 10-year-old daughter from when she was 8; she died of an infectious disease a year later.
  • The 12 Dreams in Order: (1) Multi-horned snakelike "bad animal" devours all animals, then God comes as 4 gods from the 4 corners to give rebirth; (2) Ascent to heaven where pagan dances occur, descent to hell where angels do good deeds [relativization of moral values]; (3) Small animals grow huge and devour her; (4) Small mouse penetrated by worms, snakes, fishes, and humans becomes human [4-stage creation of mankind]; (5) Drop of water under microscope full of branches [origin of world]; (6) Bad boy throws bits of earth turning passers-by bad; (7) Drunken woman falls in water and emerges sober/renewed; (8) People in America roll in ant heap, dreamer falls into river; (9) Desert on moon, sinks deep into ground to reach hell; (10) Touches luminous ball, vapors emerge, man kills her; (11) Dangerously ill, birds burst from her skin and cover her; (12) Gnats hide sun, moon, and stars except one star that falls on her.
  • Jung’s Analysis: 9 of the 12 dreams revolve around destruction and restoration (apocatastasis), alongside cosmogonic creation myths (dreams 4 & 5). Despite a superficial Protestant upbringing, her dreams contained non-Christian symbols like the divine quaternity and Gerard Dorn’s 17th-century alchemical quadricornutus serpens (four-horned serpent). Because she was approaching both puberty and early death, the dreams acted as an adumbratio—an initiatory preparation for death (like primitive puberty rites or Zen koans).
Case 3 — Origin of Reflective Consciousness (p. 116): Jung suggests reflective consciousness emerged from violent emotional shocks—such as a primitive man strangling his beloved only son in anger over catching no fish and being seized with eternal regret, or Spanish hidalgo Ramón Lull pursuing a secret lady who revealed her cancer-eaten bosom, shocking him into becoming a holy man.
Case 4 — Prognostic Fever Dreams (pp. 116–117): In the 2nd century A.D., Artemidoros of Daldis recorded a man dreaming his father burned in a house fire before dying himself of phlegmone (high fever/pneumonia). Similarly, a former patient dreamed Jung’s colleague was perishing in a great fire three weeks before the doctor died of a gangrenous fever—showing the unconscious poetically portrays the body as an earthly house destroyed by fire.
Case 5 — Mount Elgon Sunrise Ritual (p. 120): In East Africa, Mount Elgon tribesmen exit their huts at dawn and breathe or spit into their hands toward the rising sun—offering their "soul substance" (breath/spittle) to mungu (the sacred moment of sunrise). When asked why, they were baffled: "We have always done it." As Goethe’s Faust says, "Im Anfang war die Tat" ("In the beginning was the deed"): deeds come first unconsciously, reflection comes much later.
Case 6 — Modern "Compartment Psychology" (pp. 122–123): A philosophy/psychology professor who denied the unconscious became obsessed with having cancer despite clear X-rays; his conscious willpower could not stop the autonomous fear. Likewise, an alcoholic "miraculously cured" by a religious revival relapsed weeks later, and the group sent him to a medical clinic—illustrating modern dissociation between conscious reason and unconscious powers.
C. Short Answer Q1: "Primitive" vs. "Civilized" People (Strengths & Weaknesses) Toggle
Group Strengths (What They Have) Weaknesses (What They Lack / Suffer From)
So-Called "Primitive" People

• Deeply connected to the unconscious & instincts: They live their symbols emotionally rather than merely analyzing them intellectually (e.g., Mt. Elgon sunrise offering of breath/spittle to mungu).

• Intact mythic/ritual therapy: Sacred rituals and initiations provide emotional meaning and protection against existential suffering.

• Lack of conscious reflection & willpower: Cannot explain why they perform their rituals ("We have always done it"); low reflective consciousness.

• Hampered by fears & superstitions: Paralyzed at every step by doubts, taboos, spirits, and demons; require chanting/drumming just to work up energy to act.
So-Called "Civilized" People

• High willpower, rationality & efficiency: Can translate ideas into action immediately without chanting, drumming, or daily ritual prayer ("Where there's a will there's a way").

• Historical/conscious knowledge: Can trace symbolic origins (e.g., knowing more intellectually about Christmas trees than primitives know about their rites).

• Blindness to unconscious powers & lack of introspection: Hubristic illusion of total free will and self-control; severe dissociation ("compartment psychology").

• Possessed by renamed "gods and demons": Unconscious forces return as neuroses, restlessness, vague apprehensions, addictions to pills/alcohol/tobacco, and irrational phobias (e.g., cancer-phobic professor).
Module 2 Tested on: MC • T/F • Short Answer Q2 (Karma & Caste)

Leviticus & the Manusmriti (Laws of Manu)

Prof. Derynck’s Study Guide & Video Focus: More MC and T/F questions test the Manusmriti than Leviticus, though comparative questions ask how Leviticus is similar or different. Pay special attention to: (1) karma & caste justification, (2) the five most serious sins in Manu, (3) sattva / rajas / tamas applied to food purity, and (4) how both treat women and sexuality differently.

1. Karma & Caste Rationalization (Short Answer Q2)

What is Karma? In Hindu belief, karma is the cosmic law of moral cause and effect across the cycle of rebirth (samsara). Good deeds and adherence to sacred duty (dharma) yield merit leading to higher rebirth; bad deeds yield demerit leading to lower rebirth (lower caste, animal, or suffering).

How Manusmriti Justifies Caste: Manu links cosmic creation (the four varnas emerging from Purusha/Brahma: Brahmins from the mouth, Kshatriyas from arms, Vaishyas from thighs, Shudras from feet) with karma. Because a person’s birth caste reflects their past-life metaphysical merit, Brahmin superiority and social hierarchy are framed as deserved cosmic justice rather than arbitrary oppression—motivating lower castes to obey their caste duty (svadharma) for a better future rebirth.

2. Three Gunas & Food Purity (Sattva / Rajas / Tamas)

  • Sattva (Purity, Light, Goodness): Pure, nourishing, non-violent foods (e.g., milk, ghee, fruits, grains) that promote clarity, wisdom, and spiritual elevation—appropriate for Brahmins.
  • Rajas (Passion, Activity, Restlessness): Stimulating, spicy, salty, or overly rich foods that excite desire, energy, and worldly ambition.
  • Tamas (Darkness, Inertia, Impurity): Stale, decayed, foul, intoxicating, or impure foods (e.g., alcohol/sura, garlic/onions, forbidden meats) that dull the mind and cause spiritual degradation.

3. Five Most Serious Sins in Manusmriti (Mahapatakas)

According to the Manusmriti, the five mortal sins requiring severe penance are:

  1. Killing a Brahmin (Brahmahatya)
  2. Drinking intoxicating liquor (Sura, especially by twice-born castes)
  3. Stealing gold (especially from a Brahmin)
  4. Committing adultery with a guru’s/teacher’s wife
  5. Associating with anyone who commits these four grave sins

4. Leviticus vs. Manusmriti: Parallels & Divergences

  • Parallels: Both establish strict ritual purity codes (clean vs. unclean foods, bodily emissions), priestly privilege (Levites vs. Brahmins), and patriarchal control over women’s bodies and marriage.
  • Divergences (Video Highlight!): While both are problematic regarding sexuality, they differ in specifics—for instance, as Prof. Derynck notes in the video, one explicitly condemns lesbianism whereas Leviticus does not even acknowledge that lesbians exist (Leviticus focuses on male same-sex acts, bestiality, and menstrual/ritual impurity).

The Revelation to John (Chapters 1–22)

Prof. Derynck’s Video & Guide Tip: You don’t need a verse-by-verse memorization, but know everything highlighted in the slides: (1) Which of the 7 Churches are praised/blamed and why, (2) how Christ is portrayed, and (3) how the evil characters are portrayed. A. Cast of Key Figures: Christ vs. The Evil Characters Toggle

How Christ Is Portrayed (3 Major Forms)

  • 1. Son of Man Among 7 Lampstands (Ch. 1): Robe to feet, golden sash, hair white like wool/snow, eyes like blazing fire, feet like glowing bronze, voice like rushing waters, holding seven stars (angels of the 7 churches) in his right hand, and a sharp double-edged sword coming out of his mouth; holds keys of death and Hades.
  • 2. Slain Lamb / Lion of Judah (Ch. 5): Worthy to open the 7-sealed scroll; appears as a Lamb looking as if slain, standing at the center of the throne with seven horns and seven eyes (seven spirits of God).
  • 3. Conquering Rider on a White Horse (Ch. 19): Called Faithful and True, The Word of God, and KING OF KINGS AND LORD OF LORDS; eyes like blazing fire, many crowns, robe dipped in blood, sharp sword from mouth to strike down nations, rules with an iron scepter.

How the Evil Characters Are Portrayed

  • 1. The Great Red Dragon (Ch. 12): Identified as the ancient serpent, the devil, or Satan; has seven heads, ten horns, and seven crowns; tail sweeps a third of stars from the sky; tries to devour the pregnant woman’s male child; defeated by Archangel Michael and hurled to earth.
  • 2. The Beast from the Sea (Ch. 13): Given power/throne by the Dragon; has ten horns, seven heads, ten crowns, blasphemous names, body like a leopard, feet like a bear, mouth like a lion; one head had a fatal wound that was healed; wages war on God’s holy people for 42 months.
  • 3. The Beast from the Earth / False Prophet (Ch. 13): Has two horns like a lamb, but speaks like a dragon; performs deceptive signs (calling fire from heaven); animates an image of the first beast; forces everyone to receive the mark of the beast (666) on right hand or forehead to buy or sell.
  • 4. Babylon the Great / Great Prostitute (Ch. 17–18): Rides the scarlet 7-headed beast; dressed in purple, scarlet, gold, pearls; holds golden cup of abominations; drunk with the blood of God’s holy people; symbolizes imperial Rome/corrupt empire; destroyed in one hour while kings and merchants weep.

Ch. 1–3 (Exile on Patmos & 7 Letters): John is exiled on the island of Patmos for preaching Jesus. Christ appears among 7 golden lampstands and dictates messages to the 7 churches in Asia.

Ch. 4–5 (Heavenly Throne Room & 7-Sealed Scroll): John sees God’s emerald-encircled throne, 24 elders, and 4 living creatures (lion, ox, man, eagle). Only the slain Lamb is worthy to open the 7-sealed scroll.

In Ch. 7, 144,000 from Israel’s 12 tribes are sealed on their foreheads, followed by an uncountable multitude in white robes out of the great tribulation. The 7 trumpets bring ecological plagues (star Wormwood, locusts from Abyss under Abaddon/Apollyon) and the two witnesses in Jerusalem (Ch. 11) who are killed by the Beast and resurrected after 3.5 days.

In Ch. 14, the Lamb stands on Mount Zion with the 144,000, angels proclaim Babylon’s fall and eternal torment for beast-worshipers, and a heavenly voice blesses those who die in the Lord before the earth is reaped in the winepress of wrath.

Ch. 15–19 (7 Bowls of Wrath & Fall of Babylon): Seven angels pour out 7 bowls of wrath (sores, sea/rivers to blood, scorching sun, darkness, Armageddon, massive earthquake). Babylon the Great is destroyed (Ch. 17–18), followed by the Wedding Supper of the Lamb and Christ riding the white horse to cast the Beast and False Prophet into the lake of burning sulfur (Ch. 19).

Ch. 20–22 (Millennium, Judgment & New Jerusalem): Satan is bound in the Abyss for 1,000 years. In Ch. 20, beheaded martyrs who refused the beast’s mark come to life in the first resurrection to reign with Christ for 1,000 years; after Satan’s final defeat, all dead are judged before the Great White Throne by the Book of Life. In Ch. 21–22, a New Heaven, New Earth, and cube-shaped New Jerusalem (with the River and Tree of Life) descend from heaven.

Martin Luther King Jr. — Two Speeches

Prof. Derynck’s Tip: Knowing the two speech titles ("I Have a Dream" and "Paul’s Letter to American Christians") gets you the 1 MC question immediately. For the triple-value short answer (worth 6 pts!), decide ahead of time which question you are answering and put exact quotes on your cue card so your answer proves you read the speeches rather than relying on vague generalities.

Speech 1: "I Have a Dream" (1963) — Front-to-Back

  • 100 Years After Emancipation: Lincoln’s Emancipation Proclamation was a beacon of hope, yet 100 years later Black Americans remain crippled by segregation and poverty.
  • The Bad Check / Promissory Note: The Constitution and Declaration of Independence signed a promissory note guaranteeing life, liberty, and pursuit of happiness to all, but America gave Black citizens a check marked "insufficient funds." The movement comes to Washington to cash this check at the bank of justice.
  • Fierce Urgency of Now: Rejects the "tranquilizing drug of gradualism"—now is the time to rise from segregation to racial justice.
  • Nonviolent Dignity & Discipline: Warns against drinking from the "cup of bitterness and hatred" or degenerating into physical violence; urges meeting "physical force with soul force" alongside white allies whose destiny is tied to theirs.
  • Redemptive Suffering & Refusal to Be Satisfied: Cannot be satisfied amid police brutality, motel discrimination, ghettos, "whites only" signs, and disenfranchisement—"until justice rolls down like waters, and righteousness like a mighty stream."
  • The Dream & Freedom Ringing: Visions of former slaves and slave owners’ sons at the table of brotherhood, children judged by character rather than skin color, and freedom ringing from every mountainside until all sing "Free at last."

Speech 2: "Paul’s Letter to American Christians" (1958)

  • Framing Device: Delivered to the Board of Missions; MLK reads an imaginary letter written by the Apostle Paul from Crete to American Christians in 1958, noting we live in an "inescapable network of mutuality."
  • Scientific Progress vs. Moral Lag: Praises subways, airplanes, skyscrapers, and medicine, but warns America allowed "material means... to outdistance the spiritual ends," turning the world into a neighborhood without making it a brotherhood.
  • Colony of Heaven (Dual Citizenship): Condemns determining morality by a "Gallup Poll of the majority opinion." Christians live in time and eternity ("a colony of heaven") and owe ultimate allegiance to God over man-made laws.
  • Misuse of Capitalism: Warns against gross materialism (judging success by salary/car wheelbase) and extreme inequality (0.1% controlling >40% of wealth); rejects atheistic communism while urging democratic reform to wipe out poverty.
  • Divided & Segregated Church: Criticizes 256+ warring Protestant denominations, Roman Catholic claims to papal infallibility, and Sunday 11:00 AM ("the most segregated hour of Christian America"), refuting the false "curse of Ham" justification.
  • Moderation & Love (Summum Bonum): Defines true moderation as "wise restraint and calm reasonableness," whereas slowing down justice is a tragic vice. Urges nonviolence ("Put up your sword") and concludes that Love—not eloquence, knowledge, or loveless martyrdom—is the highest good (summum bonum).

MLK Short Answer Option 1: Four Cardinal Virtues

The four cardinal virtues of the ancient world are Justice, Temperance (Moderation), Courage (Fortitude), and Prudence (Wisdom). Most students pick:

• Justice: Central to both speeches—cashing the promissory note at the bank of justice, ending segregation/poverty, and quoting Amos ("until justice rolls down like waters").

• Moderation / Temperance: In Paul’s Letter, MLK explicitly distinguishes true moderation ("wise restraint and calm reasonableness") from cowardly gradualism, and demands disciplined nonviolence ("soul force," "Put up your sword").

MLK Short Answer Option 2: Sowell’s Visions

• Unconstrained Vision (Moral Optimism): MLK believes human society can fundamentally eradicate entrenched evils like racism and poverty through moral transformation, love, and democratic reform ("wipe poverty from the face of the earth").

• Constrained Vision (Flawed Nature & Divine Authority): As a Baptist minister, MLK recognizes human ego, spiritual pride ("Man has a tragic capacity to relegate a heightening virtue to a tragic vice"), and rejects human majority consensus ("Gallup Poll") in favor of eternal divine law.

Module 5 Tested on: MC • T/F • Short Answer Q6

J.S. Mill — "Utilitarianism" (Chapter II)

Prof. Derynck’s Story Framing (Video & Guide): Imagine Mill as a protagonist presenting his Greatest Happiness Principle and defending it against three antagonists: (1) critics who say he overemphasizes happiness or degrades humans to pigs, (2) critics who say his standard demands too much of ordinary people, and (3) critics who worry utilitarianism is godless/expedient and justifies ruthless behavior like Haber in The Lathe of Heaven.

1. Core Definition & Quality of Pleasures

  • Greatest Happiness Principle: Actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness (pleasure and absence of pain), wrong as they produce unhappiness (pain and privation of pleasure)—for all sentient beings, not just the agent.
  • "Doctrine Worthy Only of Swine" Objection: Critics claim pleasure as life’s end reduces humans to beasts. Mill replies that humans possess elevated intellectual, imaginative, and moral faculties.
  • Quality vs. Quantity: Pleasures differ in quality, not just quantity. Competent judges experienced in both always prefer higher faculties: "It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied."
  • Infirmity of Character: People sometimes choose nearer lower pleasures out of weakness or lose noble tastes if society/occupations starve them ("a very tender plant"), not because they genuinely judge lower pleasures superior.

2. Antagonist 1: Happiness, Sacrifice & Education

  • Is Happiness Unattainable? Critics (like Carlyle) ask "What right hast thou to be happy?" Mill clarifies happiness is not continuous rapture, but moments of rapture amid few pains, active pursuits, tranquillity and excitement, and unselfish affections.
  • What Does Mill Think of Sacrifice? Readiness to sacrifice one’s own happiness for others is the highest virtue in an imperfect world, ONLY when it increases total happiness for others. Self-sacrifice for its own sake is wasted ("no more deserving of admiration than the ascetic mounted on his pillar").
  • What Does Mill Think of Education? Wretched education and bad laws are the main obstacles to happiness. Education and public opinion should create an "indissoluble association" in every mind between one’s own happiness and the good of the whole (echoing Jesus’s Golden Rule).

3. Antagonist 2: Demanding Too Much (Rule vs. Motive)

  • Rule of Action vs. Motive: Critics claim utilitarianism demands people always act from a grand motive of serving all humanity. Mill replies this confuses the standard of duty with the motive: 99% of actions are rightly done from everyday motives.
  • Motive Does Not Determine Action’s Morality: Saving someone from drowning is morally right whether motivated by duty or hope of reward; betraying a friend is wrong even to help another friend.
  • Private vs. Public Utility: Only rare public benefactors affect society at large; ordinary people need only consider private utility (the few people directly affected), while respecting others’ rights.

4. Antagonist 3: Expediency, Veracity & Short Answer Q6

  • Long-Term Utility vs. Short-Term Expediency: Telling a lie for immediate convenience is harmful because undermining human trustworthiness damages civilization on the largest scale (with rare, strictly defined exceptions like withholding fatal news from someone dangerously ill).
  • Secondary Principles (No Time to Calculate): Humanity has had all of history to learn action tendencies, creating inherited moral rules ("landmarks" or a sailor’s Nautical Almanack) so we don’t recalculate from scratch before every act.
  • Short Answer Q6 (Unconstrained vs. Constrained in Mill): Unconstrained: human nature is malleable via education/laws, and social evils like poverty and disease are "conquerable by human care and effort." Constrained: Mill acknowledges human "infirmity of character," imperfect happiness, limits of individual calculation, and reliance on inherited "secondary principles."

Ursula K. Le Guin — "The Lathe of Heaven" (Ch. 5 end – Ch. 9+)

Prof. Derynck’s Study Guide Checklist: Tested via MC and T/F only. Remember that the tail end of Chapter 5 is on this exam! Know the major characters (George Orr, Dr. William Haber, Heather Lelache) and the exact sequence of Haber’s dream interventions and unintended disasters.

1. Haber’s "Fixes" & Unintended Results

  • Overpopulation (Tail of Ch. 5): Haber commands George to solve overpopulation → George dreams of a catastrophic plague ("The Plague") that wipes out billions, leaving a half-empty world.
  • Peace on Earth → Alien Invasion (Ch. 6–7): Haber tells George to dream of peace on Earth → George dreams of extraterrestrials invading the Moon and Earth, forcing warring human nations to unite against a common alien enemy.
  • Racial Conflict → Uniform Gray Skin (Ch. 8–9): Haber commands George to eliminate racial hatred → everyone on Earth becomes a uniform battleship-gray color (erasing diversity, and erasing Heather Lelache’s existence in the new timeline!).

2. Key Exam Details: Ch. 7, Aliens & Ch. 9

  • Chapter 7 Revelations (Lelache & Original Reality): Reveals Heather Lelache’s background (mixed-race heritage—Black father, white mother—who struggled with identity and strength) and confirms memories of the original reality (before the April 1998 nuclear apocalypse that George dreamed away at age 17).
  • The Aliens (Cause, Appearance, Speech): Caused by Haber’s "peace on Earth" command. Described as large, turtle/crab-like sea-floor beings (bulky, greenish/carapace-like, awkward on land in environmental suits) who communicate telepathically/non-verbally ("iahklu'" / dream-speak) and actually understand dreaming and the Taoist flow of reality far better than Haber.
  • Chapter 9 Drastic World Shift & Impact on Lelache: Following the "no racial conflict" dream, society becomes sterile, uniform, and gray; because Heather Lelache was born of a mixed-race marriage, she never existed in this gray timeline—devastating George, who had loved and married her in the prior reality.

Q1 (Jung: Primitive vs. Civilized)

• Primitive: live symbols emotionally; connected to unconscious; Mt. Elgon sunrise breath/spittle to mungu (soul substance); "in the beginning was the deed"; unreflective; hampered by fears/taboos/superstitions.

Q2 (Karma & Manusmriti Caste)

• Karma: cosmic moral cause-effect across rebirths (samsara); good deeds/dharma → higher rebirth; bad deeds → lower rebirth/animal.

• Manusmriti justification: 4 varnas (Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra) = earned metaphysical consequence of past lives, not arbitrary oppression; obey caste duty (svadharma) for future rebirth.

Q6 (J.S. Mill: Unconstrained vs. Constrained)

• Unconstrained: human nature malleable via education/laws; poverty & disease "conquerable by human care and effort"; morality grounded in human-calculated utility, not fixed divine decree.

• Constrained: human "infirmity of character" (choosing nearer lower pleasures); imperfect happiness; lack of time/wisdom to calculate every act → need inherited rules/"secondary principles" (landmarks / Nautical Almanack) & strict veracity.
